Executing Spot Trades in the Simulator

Prepared with AI assistance · Publication review recorded by Anuga Weerasinghe · 2026-11-02

Placing Your First Market Buy Order

To place a virtual Bitcoin market purchase, select the supported instrument and enter the intended amount. TradeHQ records the simulated transaction using its displayed practice price input, not a real exchange's executable bid or ask. The virtual cash debit includes both the purchase notional and a 0.1% simulator transaction fee. A successful simulated order increases fictional Bitcoin exposure in the account ledger rather than transferring ownership of an on-chain token. Review the recorded quantity, cash and price source after submission. These calculations are educational and cannot establish actual exchange fill quality.

As a hypothetical first-trade calculation, assume a fresh TradeHQ practice account has $100,000 virtual cash and a Bitcoin snapshot price of $50,000. Buying 0.1 virtual BTC has a $5,000 notional value; the 0.1% buy fee is $5. The simulated account would record 0.1 virtual BTC and $94,995 remaining cash, so equity at the same practice quote is $99,995. This is not actual Bitcoin ownership or an executable market offer. Compare the calculated result with the recorded ledger and check the quote's source and observation time before interpreting any difference.

Managing Spot Asset Holdings

Once your spot purchase settles, the acquired asset remains in your portfolio until you decide to execute a corresponding sale. Unlike derivative contracts, spot holdings represent a simulated token position in a virtual ledger within the context of the simulation environment. You must monitor how market price fluctuations impact the total valuation of your holdings over successive trading sessions. This ongoing observation helps you understand the direct relationship between asset quantity and aggregate portfolio worth.

A price change can alter portfolio weights. Rebalancing is one possible worksheet rule: compare it with leaving weights unchanged under the same price path, costs and chosen targets. Neither approach is universally required or guarantees a successful portfolio.

Executing Market Sell Orders

A virtual market sale reduces the recorded Bitcoin quantity and adds net proceeds to the remaining practice cash balance after a 0.1% sale fee. The simulator uses its displayed practice input, not an order-book fill from a real venue. A sale may be used to practise reconciling cash and positions, calculating a gain or loss, or comparing hypothetical outcomes under different prices. The calculation does not prove that comparable real liquidity would be available or that an actual sale would execute instantly. Keep virtual accounting and real market execution as different questions.

When preparing an exit worksheet, state the objective: for example, learning fee accounting or comparing position values under two possible price paths. A price decline alone does not mean that a sale is objectively required, and a price increase does not guarantee a better future outcome. TradeHQ does not execute conditional stop-loss or limit orders, so the learner must not assume that an exit can be scheduled at a precise price. Compare the hypothetical choice to hold against the hypothetical choice to sell after costs, and describe the uncertainty rather than prescribing a strategy.

Handling Execution Reality and Limitations

TradeHQ simplifies actual market execution. Its market orders use a displayed practice input, and each virtual buy and sale charges a 0.1% fee. The simulator does not model order-book depth, queue priority, partial fills or calibrated slippage. For a second worksheet, selling 0.1 BTC at an assumed $51,000 produces $5,100 notional less a $5.10 sale fee, or $5,094.90 proceeds. Subtracting the original $5,005 acquisition cost gives an illustrative $89.90 gain. A $49,000 sale instead gives $4,895.10 proceeds and a $109.90 loss; neither scenario predicts market outcomes.

Virtual trading can still involve real feelings, but the fictional balance does not expose the learner’s savings to market losses. That difference limits what a practice result can establish about decisions under real financial pressure. Use the exercise to check order mechanics and the cash ledger, rather than to certify emotional resilience. In a written debrief, describe what you actually observed and which circumstances the simulator left out. A confident practice decision or a completed worksheet does not demonstrate that the same response would occur when real money is at risk.

Key takeaways

  • TradeHQ's spot holdings represent fictional positions rather than direct legal ownership of Bitcoin.
  • Every buy and sale changes the virtual cash ledger by the order notional and a 0.1% virtual fee.
  • Simplified simulated fills do not reproduce exchange liquidity, slippage or emotional stakes.
